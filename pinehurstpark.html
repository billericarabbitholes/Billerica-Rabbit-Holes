Pinehurst Park

A Trolley Park, a Pine Grove, and the Hurricane of 1938

Pinehurst Park was one of Billerica’s most important recreational destinations for nearly forty years, but it is also one of the hardest to reconstruct. Opened alongside a new trolley line in 1901, it served at different times as a picnic ground, dance destination, ball field, swimming place, outing resort, and gathering place for groups from across the region. Yet its defining attraction was simpler than any of those things: a mature pine grove overlooking the Shawsheen River.

This story is divided into three parts. The first considers what Pinehurst Park actually was and why its landscape mattered. The second follows the park from its trolley-era beginnings through changing operators, audiences, and forms of entertainment. The third examines the hurricane of 1938, which destroyed not merely a collection of buildings or a seasonal business, but the landscape on which the entire place depended.

Part I

Pinehurst Park is easy to misunderstand.

At first glance, it seems like a familiar story: a trolley-era park opens, hosts picnics and dances, fades as the old outing world changes, and is finally destroyed by the 1938 hurricane.

That version is not entirely wrong. But it misses an important part.

Even the name gives it away.

Pines. A hurst — an old word for a wooded rise or grove. And a park.

The name was not hiding what the place was. This was not, first and foremost, a mechanical amusement park. It was a pine-covered park landscape: a wooded rise above the Shawsheen River, with a slope down toward the water, a swimming place, shade, open ground, and eventually the structures and entertainments that made it commercially useful.

That is harder to picture today than it sounds. A pine grove, especially a maintained one, is not just “some trees.” A mature grove has a particular feeling: tall trunks of Eastern white pines shoot toward the sky, while the dense canopy knits together and plunges the grove into a cool, perpetual twilight. The forest floor isn’t covered in twigs, crunching leaves, or weeds. It is buried under a thick, rusty-red blanket of millions of fallen pine needles accumulated over decades. Walking on it feels like walking on a plush, heavy rug.

Early descriptions repeatedly emphasize shade, cool air, towering pines, and the riverbank itself. In an era before widespread air conditioning, places like this carried real physical value. A mature pine grove along the river would have felt dramatically different.

But for all that, we do not have a detailed description of Pinehurst Park’s grove. We cannot reconstruct exactly how dense it was, how tall the trees were, or how the paths moved through it.

The surviving record is frustratingly thin. Pinehurst was not the kind of place that left behind a detailed architectural archive or a rich photographic record. There are no surviving park maps that I’ve found, if any ever existed in the first place. Most of what survives comes from classified ads, short newspaper notices, outing reports, and promotional language. The photographs that have surfaced are few, and many show the entrance rather than the interior life of the park itself. If better images exist somewhere, they are buried in family albums, church archives, fraternal society scrapbooks, or boxes that have not been opened in decades.

So it is difficult to reconstruct Pinehurst exactly. The descriptions are often short on specifics and long on praise. But even allowing for advertising exaggeration, one theme keeps coming through over the decades: people thought the place was beautiful.

Not a seventh wonder of the world. Not a grand amusement resort. But legitimately beautiful enough to build a regional draw around. Beautiful enough for Boston churches, Lowell groups, political organizations, employee outings, summer residents, and local families to keep coming back. Beautiful enough that the same basic property remained useful for nearly forty years with that as its defining characteristic.

Pinehurst was not famous because it was grand. It was famous, to the extent it was famous at all, because it was useful and pretty at the right scale. It was worth the day trip. Billerica was not given towering mountains, waterfalls, or dramatic scenery. Its beauty was and remains quieter: rivers, groves, woodlands, meadows, shaded roads, and small places that feel set apart from ordinary life. Pinehurst Park seems to have been a shining example of one of those places.

A modern comparison is imperfect, but useful: I imagine Pinehurst Park was probably less like a lost Canobie Lake Park and more like a lost Kimball Farm. Not Kimball as a ride park, but Kimball as a regional day-trip place — food, open air, simple amusements, group outings, and enough to do that families, clubs, companies, churches, and friends could make a day of it. Flexible enough that you could make the day whatever you wanted it to be. Pinehurst’s version was older and more landscape-based: pine grove, river, swimming hole, swings, ball field, cafe, dance pavilion, music, and shade — but conceptually, we are there.

That is the point. Pinehurst was a place-based recreation business built around a landscape that already had value. The pavilion helped bring crowds, but the grove made the park.

Jumping ahead briefly — that is what makes the ending so important. The 1938 hurricane did not merely damage a business. It leveled the landscape that made the business possible.

But before that, for almost forty years, Pinehurst Park was something harder to picture today: a maintained riverside landscape whose main attraction was the land itself. That is the version we will try to recover.

Part II

The story begins on September 3, 1901, when the Lowell & Boston Street Railway opened its new trolley line to Woburn Centre by way of Burlington. Pinehurst Park opened the same day.

The newspaper reports announcing the trolley line and the park are the same reports. From the beginning, the two were linked. Pinehurst Park opened as a trolley park in the most literal sense: a destination created to give people a reason to ride.

But it was not an amusement park in the modern, purpose-built sense. The original descriptions emphasize the landscape more than the attractions. A pavilion stood atop a hill in a pine grove overlooking the Shawsheen River. Below were a bandstand, refreshment booths, swings, rows of benches beneath the trees, and a spring of cool water. The trolley ride itself was part of the attraction, promising views of farms, hills, countryside, and fresh air.

The first ambitions look larger than the park Pinehurst later became. Early advertisements promoted “refined vaudeville,” brass and cornet bands, novelty acts, and destination dining. At one point, the park advertised a chef associated with Brown University and specialty fish dinners. Early Pinehurst was trying to feel respectable, current, and excursion-worthy.

But those ambitions did not last.

The trolley line was fragile almost from the beginning. The Lowell & Boston was never a major regional corridor and was wildly undercapitalized. Pinehurst’s fate was tied to a line with limited reach, limited resources, and recurring instability. In a pre-automobile world, the park could not succeed without it.

The ownership and operating picture complicates the story further. The trolley company appears to have created and perhaps operated the first incarnation, but it never owned the underlying land.

After the Lowell & Boston’s bankruptcy and absorption into the Boston & Northern system, the line gained some stability and Pinehurst entered what appears to have been a second phase. By 1906 the park was being handled through leases and outside proprietors, while advertisements began promoting a “New Pinehurst Park.”

That seems to mark a real shift. The trolley-created park became a locally operated pleasure park offering picnics, dances, concerts, baseball, church outings, social-club gatherings, moving pictures, and whatever else a proprietor believed might draw a crowd that season. Advertising also became more Lowell-focused for regular events such as dancing, while Boston promotion was increasingly limited to organized outings and group functions.

William P. Adams of Boston is the first significant figure in this phase. He leased the buildings at Pinehurst, including the dance hall, banquet room, boating facilities, and other structures connected to “picnic parties and trolley business.”

That phrase captures the relationship perfectly. The park and the trolley were connected, but they were not the same enterprise.

Pinehurst was not a fixed amusement park with a permanent collection of rides and attractions. It was a seasonal place reconstructed from year to year. Different operators could mean different food, vendors, bands, attractions, and crowds. Two seasons that look similar in the newspapers may have felt very different on the ground.

The record also presents a basic problem: it is not always clear whether something proposed, advertised, or licensed actually happened.

Boxing, for example, appears as a proposal but apparently never took place. Wrestling, presumably professional, progressed further and seems to have occurred at least once. A merry-go-round appeared for a few early seasons and then vanished. Outdoor bowling surfaced repeatedly throughout the park’s history, but it is difficult to know whether the attraction itself came and went or whether only the advertising did.

That uncertainty is part of the story. Pinehurst was a place of permissions, experiments, seasonal adjustments, and practical improvisation. Some attractions became central. Others were short-lived. Some may never have advanced beyond a hearing, license, or advertisement. In some years, one proprietor controlled the entire park. In others, separate operators held licenses for the dance hall, refreshments, and other parts of the business.

By the 1910s Pinehurst was no longer a trolley-company operation in any direct legal sense. It was now a park on the trolley line. But the advertisements’ promise — “Take Woburn Car from Merrimack Sq. Every 30 Minutes” — was not merely a transportation detail. It was the park’s lifeblood.

Advertisements told people which car to take, how often it ran, and how much the fare cost. Special cars were arranged for outings. Late cars returned dancers to Lowell on special nights. If trolley service was unreliable, infrequent, or financially strained, Pinehurst immediately felt the effects.

Between roughly 1913 and 1915, the park seems to have reached something close to its mature peak. It was not the grandest amusement park in Massachusetts, but it was busy, flexible, and deeply embedded in the region’s social life. It hosted parish outings, political rallies, athletic contests, baseball games, dances, concerts, picnics, and organized excursions. Groups came from Lowell, Woburn, Lexington, Burlington, Arlington, Winchester, Cambridge, Somerville, Boston, and beyond.

The trolley still mattered. Special cars still mattered. Pinehurst still depended on moving organized groups into a place that was otherwise not especially convenient to reach.

The entertainment evolved constantly. Early Pinehurst featured regional touring acts, moving pictures, comedians, musical performances, and brass bands. Over time, the park shifted more heavily toward dancing, baseball, outings, and social gatherings. The dance hall increasingly became the park’s center of gravity, at least in newspaper coverage and town licensing hearings.

It is easy to flatten all of that into “old-time music,” but a cornet-band concert in 1901 and a jazz-era dance in the 1920s belonged to very different cultural worlds.

Young people at the turn of the twentieth century were no more eager to dance to their parents’ music than young people are today. So when Pinehurst appears again and again in the papers as a dance destination, that continuity can be deceptive. The building may have been the same, and the word “dance” may have been the same, but the experience inside could change dramatically from one season to the next.

Baseball underwent a similar transformation. In the earlier years, games were promoted as part of the park’s entertainment: another reason to take the trolley, spend the afternoon, and remain for the larger outing. A ballgame belonged naturally beside the picnic, band concert, refreshments, and dance.

By the 1920s, that relationship had changed. Baseball still took place at Pinehurst, but in the newspaper record it increasingly resembles ordinary local sport being played on the grounds rather than programming intended to draw people to the park.

The game remained. The excursion culture around it was fading.

That is another central difficulty in writing about Pinehurst Park. The name stayed the same, but the lived experience changed continually.

On any given day, Pinehurst could be different things to different people: a church picnic ground in the afternoon, a baseball field in the evening, and a dance destination at night, sometimes until four in the morning. It hosted family outings, ethnic and fraternal societies, parish events, women’s baseball, field days, campers, bungalow residents, political gatherings from both sides of the aisle, and late-night dances.

The First World War years seem to have slowed the park’s momentum. Parades, outings, and large social gatherings appeared less frequently, or at least less prominently, in the newspapers. By 1920, references to an “old-fashioned picnic” at Pinehurst suggest that the park was still active but may already have felt slightly old-fashioned itself.

The clearest break came in 1921, when trolley service gave way to buses. The trolley’s usefulness had already been declining for several years, but the buses made the change final. Their schedules were built around ordinary transportation needs, not excursion parties, revelers, or late-night dancers.

Often that would have ended the story. Once the trolley disappeared, the trolley park disappeared with it.

Pinehurst survived, but it was no longer a trolley park. Whatever connection remained to the broader trolley-excursion world was gone.

After 1921, the park settled into a smaller but durable orbit. Its world was now Lowell, Billerica, Woburn, Burlington, and the growing summer communities around Pinehurst itself: bungalow developments, camping lots, seasonal residents, local social clubs, dancers, baseball crowds, and nearby church organizations.

One figure who should not disappear from the story is J. Joseph McHugh. McHugh held title to Pinehurst for decades and operated the park himself during the first part of that ownership. Those appear to have been among Pinehurst’s strongest years.

But his attention was increasingly pulled elsewhere. A 1924 account stated directly that the pressure of his real estate business had kept him for several years from giving the park the time and attention it needed during the summer season.

The effects were already visible in 1923, when new lessees promised to operate Pinehurst as a “clean and respectable resort.” That language suggests the town had become wary of what the park might become without firmer oversight.

Walter H. Chandler of North Billerica and Edward Fahey of Lowell planned to operate the dance hall, refreshment booth, and moving-picture entertainments. They also promised to work with police to “eliminate any objectionable features that have existed there in past years.”

By the 1930s, the old energy had thinned. The same kinds of groups still appeared, but less frequently.

Yet the park was not dead.

In 1935, William Blomberg of Newton proposed an animal park at Pinehurst and reportedly arranged to lease part of the property. The selectmen considered the application premature and wanted more information before granting the necessary amusement permit.

The proposal is useful even if it went nowhere. It shows Pinehurst being tested for new purposes. The park was no longer a confident trolley-era resort. It had become a flexible landscape that could be leased, reconsidered, and reimagined.

Then came the most interesting proposal of all.

In August 1938, McHugh sought approval for an “open-air auto theatre” at Pinehurst. This was not a vague or fanciful idea. According to the Lowell Sun, the theatre was to be modeled on the Merrimack Park Auto Theater in Methuen, which had opened that same summer.

Auto theatres were still a novelty in 1938. The Methuen theatre was reportedly only the fourteenth in the nation. Pinehurst, born as a trolley park in 1901, was being considered for one of the newest forms of automobile entertainment.

In hindsight, the idea seems almost prophetic.

Exactly what McHugh intended to build remains unclear. The surviving notices do not describe the layout, scale, or construction. There is no evidence that the plan required clearing the entire pine grove, and such a dramatic alteration probably would have attracted notice. More plausibly, the theatre would have occupied an existing open or semi-open part of the park, perhaps a field or already-developed section, with whatever additional clearing and grading were necessary.

McHugh never built the auto theatre, for reasons that were not reported. But he had identified the property’s next possible life before it arrived. He eventually sold the land in 1951, and the Pinehurst Drive-In opened there in 1952.

That later drive-in was not a direct continuation of the 1938 idea. By 1952, the drive-in had become a more established postwar business model. But the basic idea had already been on the table: if Pinehurst could no longer draw crowds by trolley, perhaps it could draw them by car.

The auto-theatre proposal also should not be mistaken for proof that Pinehurst Park was already dead. It was still operating during the summer of 1938. McHugh was reconsidering the property and looking toward automobile-based entertainment, but the old park had not yet disappeared. As late as August and September, Pinehurst was still appearing in the newspapers as an active place for outings, dancing, and summer recreation.

Part III

The hurricane of 1938 was not an ordinary New England storm.

If you have lived in Massachusetts your entire life, you have probably never seen a storm like this one.

Even using the word hurricane in relation to Massachusetts feels unusual. But the hurricane of 1938 was not just another bad storm. It was one of the defining weather disasters in New England history. As far as hurricanes here go, 1938 stands almost alone, rivaled only by the great colonial hurricane of 1635.

Hurricanes were as unfamiliar to New Englanders then as they are now. So unfamiliar, in fact, that after the storm passed, the chief of the Boston Weather Bureau felt compelled to reassure the public that it would not come back.

It struck before modern forecasting, before most people understood what was coming, and it did not merely damage buildings or flood roads. It flattened mature trees across an enormous area.

Billerica was hit hard. The town did not experience the storm’s highest winds, worst flooding, or greatest loss of life, but those are not distinctions worth winning. The fact that Billerica apparently escaped without a fatality — remembered afterward as “a miracle” — should not make the destruction sound mild.

The hurricane was later described as leaving “a trail of damage unprecedented in the town’s history,” with reported losses exceeding $10 million in modern dollars.

Contemporary reports described a town knocked essentially out of operation. On September 22, the Lowell Sun reported that “all streets in the town of Billerica were blocked,” the lights were out, telephone communication was impossible, and trees had been uprooted along Andover, Concord, and Boston Roads, with some striking houses.

The next day, the damage still looked overwhelming. The paper reported that homes had been split, crushed, or damaged by fallen trees; apple orchards were “practically wiped out”; and henhouses had been blown “distances of several hundred feet.” Town highway workers, aided by WPA men, were trying to clear fallen trees and poles from the roads, but the work was slow. It was expected to take days before all streets were passable.

Pinehurst was among the hardest-hit sections of town.

Although crews had worked through the day and night, a September 23 report declared that “Pinehurst still looks like a war torn district.” The neighborhood remained in darkness. Electric poles and wires were down, “huge logs” continued to block roads, and parts of Pinehurst had been “practically isolated from the rest of the town.”

For several days, the normal geography of Billerica stopped working. Roads no longer connected people in the ordinary way. The town had to be cut open again with axes and saws.

By September 24, the Concord River was finally receding after creating a flood menace for two days, though “acres and acres of lowland” remained underwater. Hundreds of WPA workers and town employees were still chopping trees and clearing debris. The town remained without electricity, leaving homes without electric cooking or heat and stores struggling without refrigeration.

One report reached for the title of a popular song of the day to capture the scene: “The entire town resembles ‘Lamp Lighting Time in the Valley,’ with every home lighted by oil lamps and candles.”

The return to lamplight extended beyond homes: places like police headquarters and the fire station were also using old-fashioned lamps.

The paper concluded that Billerica had been “set back about 50 years.”

Later local histories remembered the disruption as one measured in weeks — not days. Electric service failed shortly after 5 p.m. on September 21. House lights and telephone service were not fully restored until late October, about thirty days later, and streetlights did not return until November.

That was the condition of the town when people began making their way to Pinehurst to see what had happened to the old grove.

A few days after the hurricane, the Lowell Sun reported unusually heavy traffic through Billerica as sightseers and returning summer residents came to inspect the destruction. “Scores of people thronged Pinehurst Park,” the paper wrote, because it had long been “famous for many years for its magnificent pine trees and ideal picnic grounds.”

What they found was no longer the Pinehurst they remembered.

There was “nothing left now of the once beautiful grove but a few scraggy trees and hundreds of huge logs.”

That was the end of Pinehurst Park — not because every structure disappeared immediately, or because no one ever walked the land again, but because the thing that made Pinehurst Pinehurst had been erased.

The park was never simply a collection of buildings and amusements. Its central attraction was older than the business itself: the Shawsheen River, the swimming place, the open picnic grounds, and above all the mature pine grove. Pinehurst Park had not created that landscape so much as adapted it, giving a place people already valued a more formal recreational purpose.

So when the hurricane struck Pinehurst, it did not just damage a seasonal amusement business. It destroyed the thing the business had been built around.

Many lost places disappear in familiar ways. They are sold, subdivided, paved over, neglected, burned, or deliberately replaced. Pinehurst’s ending was different. It was not first razed for development, quietly abandoned by a failing owner, or displaced by a planned new use.

Nature ended it first.

What came afterward belonged to a different age, and the drive-in has a history of its own. But the story of Pinehurst Park closes in September 1938, when one of the most powerful storms in New England memory tore through Billerica and reduced its famous pine grove to logs.

The hurricane did not simply close the park. It removed the reason the park had existed.

Written by Keith Lavon.
